## 1. Saving

**Where it lives today.** Bookmarks v2 are items plus a folder tree (`SK.BOOKMARKS`, `SK.BM_FOLDERS`). History is separate. Signed-in accounts already keep bookmarks, folders, history and preferences on the server (`users.py`, per-user data, merged with `_mergeByKey`); without an account everything stays in the browser. Bookshelf keeps reading positions in the browser only (`zimi_book_places`). The apps hide the reader's bookmark and history buttons, so a saved video, question or place has no natural home.

**The model.** One saved item: what it is (`article`, `book`, `video`, `question`, `post`, `place`), the ZIM and path, a title, the app it belongs to, and where you were in it (a section, a book position, a video time, a map view). Lists are named groups, and an item can be in several (a "Portugal trip" list holds a Wikivoyage guide, a map place and a TED talk). Built in: Liked (the parked like button, as a list), and Continue (the last position per book or video, derived, not a list).

**Highlights** use one engine in every reader (articles, books, Zimipedia): select text, save it with a note, find it again in the panel and in the page, kept with the account like the rest.

**Where you see it.**
- Each app shows its own slice in place: Bookshelf's My shelf and Continue reading, ZimiTube's Watch later, ZimiExchange's and Reddot's Saved, Maps' Places, Zimipedia's reading lists.
- The shared panel shows everything, opens on the current app's slice, and holds the lists.
- A list can be exported as a ZIM (the bookmarks export, per list).

**Sync.** Signed-in: on the server, across devices, as today. Signed-out: in the browser, as today. Bookshelf's positions move into the same store, so Continue reading follows the account.

**Migration.** Existing bookmarks become saved items; each folder becomes a list, keeping order and nesting names. Nothing is lost, and the old keys stay readable for one release.

**Tests.** Migration round-trips real v2 exports; merge across two devices; each app's save and reopen, landing where you were; the export per list.

## 2. Zimipedia v2

Held out of 1.11 (opt-in only). It becomes an encyclopedia that owns the article.
- **The article page:** Bookshelf's reading settings (theme, font, size, margins), the lead image, a contents list that follows you, the infobox beside the text on desktop and folded on a phone, bars that hide while reading.
- **Languages by Q-ID:** only languages that have this article, landing on the same section; two side by side on desktop; Simple English as a reading level when installed.
- **One topic, every wiki:** from the Q-ID, a strip of what else the library has: the Wiktionary entry, the Wikivoyage guide, Wikiquote, Wikibooks, a map for coordinates.
- **Diving in:** tap a link for a preview card (first sentence and picture) and choose to follow it; a trail of where you came through; read next from the lead.
- **Saving:** reading lists and highlights, from part 1's shared engine.
- **Today shrinks** to a front door: continue reading, recent trails, the day's article.
- Then Zimipedia goes back on by default.

## 4. Almanac: the solar system you can fly, down to the Earth's satellites

Eric, 2026-09-27: "I'm imagining a view first actually you hover and that should show the radio delay and have a way to I guess click a glow around the earth then zoom into that scale to see earth moon ISS AND ALSO GPS satellites for that. It could be a full 3D earth. With proper sun exposure etc. accurate during eclipses. Drag the whole thing around. And way more."

**The orrery first.** Hover (or tap) a planet: its distance and the radio delay to it right now ("a message to Mars takes 12 min 40 s"). Tap it: ride the rocket there, the planets where they really are today, the delay home growing as you go.

**A glow around the Earth zooms in to the Earth's own scale:** a 3D Earth lit by the real Sun (day, night and the terminator where they are now), the Moon where it is, the ISS and the GPS constellation on their orbits. Drag to turn it; pinch or scroll to zoom. Eclipses are right: the Moon's shadow crosses the Earth during a solar eclipse, and the Earth's shadow darkens the Moon during a lunar one, from the same geometry the Almanac already computes.

**The relativity demonstrations live in these views**, each one picture that moves and one sentence that explains, numbers computed live:
1. **GPS:** tap a GPS satellite; its clock gains 38 microseconds a day (+45 from weaker gravity, -7 from speed), a counter runs from when you tapped, and what ignoring it would cost (about 10 km a day).
2. **The twin paradox**, on the rocket: a speed slider up to 0.99c, the ship's clock beside Earth's (to Proxima at 0.9c: 4.7 years on Earth, about 2 aboard).
3. **The light clock:** a photon between two mirrors, at rest and moving; the moving path is longer, so its ticks are slower.
4. **Muons:** faint streaks in the sky scene; a muon lives 2.2 microseconds and still reaches the ground from 15 km up (Feynman's example).
Each links to Wikipedia's article via Q-ID when installed.

**Offline and honest.**
- The 3D view needs a WebGL library served from Zimi itself (three.js is MIT) and an Earth texture (NASA's Blue Marble is public domain); both ship in the package, sized for a phone.
- Satellites (Eric, 2026-09-27: "maybe there should be parts that are online or offline for almanac and that is a queried thing that if comes in can be drawn otherwise isn't?"). The catalog's pattern: ship a snapshot, refresh it when online, never wait on the network; `ZIMI_OFFLINE` turns the refresh off.
  - Pure math, exact offline forever: the Earth's day and night, the Moon, eclipses, the planets.
  - GPS: real positions from orbital data shipped with each release, good for months at this scale, refreshed when online.
  - ISS: its orbit (height, tilt, 92-minute lap) is always right. Its place along the orbit is exact with fresh data; with stale data the dot fades and says so ("approximate: orbital data from 12 Sep").
  - No invented satellites: a made-up object would undercut an Almanac that is otherwise exact.
- This is the first step of the 2.0 "drivable solar system" (one scene graph and camera from the Moon to any planet); the rest of that stays 2.0.
